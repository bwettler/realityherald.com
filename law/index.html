AREAS OF FOCUS

What Reality Herald Law will cover

Christian Legal Thought

Arguments about law, justice, rights, authority, conscience, family, political obligation, and the role Christian convictions may play in legal reasoning.

Natural Law

Classical and contemporary natural-law arguments, criticisms of them, and disputes about human nature, moral realism, goods, duties, and legal limits.

Religious Liberty

Free exercise, establishment, conscience, compelled speech, institutional autonomy, church governance, and conflicts among constitutional rights.

Church & State

Jurisdiction, establishment, incorporation, religious institutions, governmental authority, and the history behind modern church-state doctrine.

Text, history, precedent, originalism, living constitutionalism, judicial power, stare decisis, incorporation, and competing theories of constitutional authority.

Family & Education Law

Parental authority, schooling, religious education, student rights, curriculum disputes, educational pluralism, and the legal governance of formation.

MAJOR CASES

Cases worth knowing

1803

Marbury v. Madison

Judicial review and the place of courts in constitutional interpretation.

1947

Everson v. Board of Education

Incorporation of the Establishment Clause and the modern judicial language of church-state separation.

1962

Engel v. Vitale

Government-composed prayer in state schools and the reach of the Establishment Clause.

1963

Abington School District v. Schempp

Bible reading, devotional exercises, and the constitutional treatment of religion in state schools.

2022

Carson v. Makin

Religious schools, shared educational benefits, and Free Exercise Clause limits on exclusion.

2022

Kennedy v. Bremerton School District

Religious expression by government employees and the Court's rejection of the Lemon structure.

FOR STUDENTS

Learn how legal arguments are built

Read cases

Start with the question presented, holding, governing rule, and the majority's reason.

Compare opinions

Read concurrences and dissents as rival legal arguments, not merely as political positions.

Trace precedent

Follow cited cases backward and ask how later courts extend, narrow, or distinguish them.

Identify first principles

Ask which claims about rights, authority, equality, conscience, human nature, or institutional competence do the real work.
