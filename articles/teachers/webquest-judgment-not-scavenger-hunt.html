A WebQuest Should End in Judgment, Not a Scavenger Hunt

Online inquiry works best when students must evaluate evidence and defend a conclusion.

RH
Reality Herald Editorial Desk September 2026 · 5 min read
Filed under IdeasHistory & Theology
Article type Teaching Guide

A WebQuest becomes educationally weak when the assignment amounts to an online scavenger hunt: visit five pages, copy five facts, submit a worksheet.

The strongest WebQuests use curated online sources to move students toward judgment. Students should finish knowing not only more information, but also how to compare sources, identify competing claims, and defend a conclusion.

Begin with a problem, not a topic

“The Civil War” is a topic. “Which explanation best accounts for secession in 1860–61?” is a problem. “Renewable energy” is a topic. “Which energy mix should this city adopt under these cost and reliability constraints?” is a problem.

A question gives research direction and creates a reason to distinguish strong evidence from merely interesting information.

Curate disagreement

Teachers should not assemble only sources that point toward one predetermined answer. A serious WebQuest gives students access to competing evidence or interpretations while maintaining standards of credibility.

That does not require false balance. A fringe source does not deserve equal weight merely because it disagrees. The teacher's job involves choosing materials that expose real disputes and different forms of evidence.

Make source evaluation part of the task

Students should ask who produced a source, what the source can establish, which audience it addresses, and what interests or assumptions may shape it. A government report, advocacy brief, newspaper article, scholarly paper, and primary document can all prove useful while serving very different purposes.

Require a product that forces judgment

The final task might ask students to write an argument, prepare a debate, recommend a policy, create a Model UN position, compare historical interpretations, or defend a legal conclusion. The product should require students to use evidence rather than merely display it.

Grade reasoning explicitly

If the rubric rewards only completion, students learn that research means collecting information. Include criteria for thesis, evidence quality, treatment of counterarguments, source use, accuracy, and reasoning.

A successful WebQuest therefore ends somewhere different from where it began. Students enter with a question and leave having made a judgment they can defend.

Teacher prompt: Before assigning a WebQuest, ask: “What judgment will students have to make that they cannot make responsibly without doing the research?”
