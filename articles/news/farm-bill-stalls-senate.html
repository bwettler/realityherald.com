Farm Bill Stalls in Senate as SNAP Fight Threatens Another Extension

The House has passed a five-year bill. Senate negotiations remain stuck over how quickly states should begin sharing the cost of food-aid benefits when payment error rates run high.

RH
Reality Herald Editorial DeskSeptember 8, 2026 · 7 min read
Filed underNews & Analysis
Article typeREPORT / ANALYSIS
StatusAs of Sept. 8, 2026

Congress is approaching another farm-bill deadline with a major piece of agricultural legislation still stalled in the Senate. The House passed its version in April, but the Senate Agriculture Committee failed on August 6 to advance Chairman John Boozman's proposal after Democrats opposed the measure. The immediate dispute centers on the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, or SNAP, and a new requirement that states with high payment error rates begin sharing part of the cost of benefits.

The current extension of remaining mandatory farm-bill programs expires September 30, 2026, or at the end of the 2026 crop year, depending on the program. Many of the largest commodity programs already run farther into the future because Congress extended them separately in 2025. That means a missed deadline would not make federal farm policy disappear overnight. But another extension would leave Congress operating from an aging 2018 law while delaying decisions on programs that farmers, food-aid administrators, rural communities, and other constituencies want updated.

What the House has already done

The House passed the Farm, Food, and National Security Act of 2026 on April 30 by a vote of 224-200. Fourteen Democrats joined Republicans in supporting the bill. House Agriculture Committee Chairman Glenn "GT" Thompson called the vote a step toward updated policy for farmers and rural communities and urged the Senate to act.

The Senate has taken a different route. Boozman, an Arkansas Republican who chairs the Senate Agriculture Committee, released an updated "Farm Bill 2.0" proposal at the end of July. The draft includes year-round sales of E15 gasoline, changes to farm and rural programs, and revisions designed to give states more time to prepare for the new SNAP cost-sharing rules enacted in 2025.

What Republicans and Democrats are fighting about

The sharpest disagreement concerns SNAP administration. Under current law, states with sufficiently high payment error rates can eventually become responsible for part of benefit costs. Republicans describe that structure as an accountability mechanism: states that administer the program poorly should not be insulated indefinitely from the financial consequences of high error rates.

Boozman's revised proposal offered states additional flexibility, including allowing them to use either fiscal-year 2026 or 2027 error-rate data when calculating the benefit cost share that would apply in fiscal year 2029. He also proposed additional funding for emergency food purchases and argued that his offer already represented a substantial concession to Democratic concerns.

Democrats, led on the committee by Minnesota Sen. Amy Klobuchar, say the Republican adjustment does not give every state a fair transition. Klobuchar argues that a one-year fix can favor some states with very high error rates over states that have already brought their error rates down but remain above the threshold. Democrats have sought a two-year delay so states begin from the same timetable while trying to improve administration.

The August 6 committee vote failed 10-11. Boozman accused Democrats of blocking a bill containing more than 100 bipartisan measures. Klobuchar responded that Democrats still want a farm bill and support provisions including year-round E15, but remain unwilling to accept the proposed SNAP timetable.

Why SNAP can hold up a farm bill

The farm bill has never dealt only with farms. The legislation traditionally combines commodity programs, crop insurance, conservation, rural development, research, nutrition assistance, forestry, trade, and other policies. Nutrition programs account for more than 70 percent of projected federal spending covered by the farm-and-nutrition baseline for 2027 through 2036.

That combination also creates the coalition that has historically helped farm bills pass. Agricultural lawmakers want support for producers and rural economies. Lawmakers with large food-aid constituencies want nutrition programs protected. When either side believes the bargain has shifted too far, the entire bill can stall.

Can Congress still pass a full bill?

Yes, but the calendar works against a quick resolution. The House and Senate ultimately must approve the same legislative text before a bill can go to the president. The Senate Agriculture Committee has not advanced Boozman's proposal, and a durable Senate package would likely need bipartisan support to overcome procedural obstacles on the floor.

The disagreement is narrower than a complete partisan breakdown over agricultural policy. Both Boozman and Klobuchar say they want a bill. Both sides support a number of the same farm provisions. The committee even adopted additional bipartisan amendments during the August meeting. But the remaining SNAP dispute matters enough to stop the package.

That makes a complete five-year bill before September 30 possible but increasingly difficult. Congress has only a short legislative window, and lawmakers also face election-year pressures. A negotiated agreement later in the fall remains plausible if the two sides find a compromise on the SNAP transition.

What happens if Congress misses September 30?

The most likely immediate response would be another extension, not the sudden disappearance of farm programs.

Congress has already extended the 2018 Farm Bill multiple times. The 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act separately extended many of the largest programs, including Agriculture Risk Coverage and Price Loss Coverage, through 2031. A later continuing-appropriations law extended remaining mandatory programs with budget baselines through September 30, 2026, or the end of the 2026 crop year.

Programs whose authority or mandatory funding depends on the expiring extension could face uncertainty or lapse unless Congress acts again. Agencies, states, producers, and program participants would also have to keep planning around temporary law rather than a new five-year settlement.

Congress could pass another short-term extension, attach one to a larger spending measure, or continue negotiations after the deadline. That approach would keep the system operating while postponing the central policy dispute.

The most likely outcome

At this point, another extension looks more likely than enactment of a complete five-year farm bill by September 30. That assessment could change quickly if Boozman and Klobuchar strike a SNAP compromise, because much of the agricultural package already has bipartisan support.

Failure to meet the September deadline therefore would not necessarily signal that the farm bill has collapsed. More likely, Congress would buy additional time. The larger question is whether lawmakers can use that time to replace a law written in 2018 rather than extending it yet again.
