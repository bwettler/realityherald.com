The Argument Beneath the Argument

Why people can agree on the evidence and still reach radically different conclusions.

EG
Eleanor Grant September 2, 2026 · 8 min read
Filed under News & Analysis Ideas
Article type Analysis

Two people can agree on a surprising number of facts and still reach opposite conclusions. That outcome often looks irrational only because the deeper disagreement remains hidden.

Public arguments usually begin in the middle. Participants cite statistics, historical events, legal texts, scientific findings, or personal experience. Each side then accuses the other of ignoring the evidence. Yet the dispute may not concern the evidence alone. People also bring judgments about which facts matter, how competing facts should be weighed, what counts as a valid inference, and which moral principles should govern the conclusion.

The argument beneath the argument

Suppose two people agree that a policy will produce both benefits and costs. One person may regard individual liberty as the controlling consideration. Another may give greater weight to equality, social stability, parental authority, public safety, or some other good. Neither person necessarily denies the same empirical findings. Their disagreement concerns the principles by which those findings should be evaluated.

The same phenomenon appears in debates about science. Observations never arrive accompanied by a complete interpretation of their significance. Researchers work within theories, methods, definitions, and background assumptions. Those structures do not make empirical inquiry arbitrary, but they do remind us that facts and interpretations should not be collapsed into one category.

Disagreement about conclusions may reveal disagreement about the standards by which conclusions are drawn.

Why naming assumptions matters

Arguments become clearer when participants identify the premises doing the real work. A debate about education, for example, may appear to concern curriculum. Beneath that question may lie competing accounts of parental authority, the purpose of schooling, human nature, moral formation, or the proper reach of government.

Likewise, a disagreement about historical interpretation can turn on what kinds of sources deserve priority, how much weight later testimony should receive, or whether an author's stated motive should control our understanding of an institution's effects.

None of this means that every assumption has equal merit. Quite the opposite. Once assumptions become visible, they can themselves become subjects of argument. We can ask whether they cohere, whether they explain the evidence, whether they contradict other commitments, and whether their consequences can withstand scrutiny.

Facts still matter

Recognizing background assumptions does not reduce everything to perspective. Some claims remain false. Some evidence remains stronger than other evidence. Some arguments contain contradictions or invalid inferences. A serious publication should therefore resist two opposite errors: pretending facts interpret themselves, and pretending interpretation makes facts irrelevant.

Better disagreement requires both empirical discipline and intellectual candor. We should ask not only, “What evidence supports this conclusion?” but also, “What premises connect the evidence to the conclusion?” That second question often reveals why intelligent people can stare at the same record and see very different things.
