The Argument Beneath the Argument

Why people can agree on the evidence and still reach radically different conclusions.

RH
Reality Herald Editorial Desk September 2026 · 6 min read
Filed under IdeasNews & Analysis
Article type Analysis

Two people can agree on a surprising number of facts and still reach opposite conclusions. That outcome often looks irrational only because the deeper disagreement remains hidden.

Arguments usually begin in the middle. Participants cite statistics, historical events, legal texts, scientific findings, or personal experience. Each side then accuses the other of ignoring the evidence. Yet the dispute may concern more than evidence. People also bring judgments about which facts matter, what counts as a valid inference, and which moral principles should govern the conclusion.

The premises that do the real work

Suppose two people agree that a proposed policy will produce both benefits and costs. One gives individual liberty controlling weight. Another gives greater weight to equality, social stability, parental authority, public safety, or some other good. Neither person necessarily denies the same empirical findings. Their disagreement concerns the principles by which those findings should be evaluated.

The same pattern appears in science, history, law, and theology. Evidence never arrives with a complete interpretation attached. Researchers choose methods, historians weigh sources, judges apply legal rules, and theologians make judgments about texts and doctrines. Those judgments can be better or worse, but they remain judgments.

Disagreement about conclusions may reveal disagreement about the standards by which conclusions are drawn.

Why naming assumptions matters

Arguments improve when participants identify the premises doing the real work. A dispute about education may appear to concern curriculum while depending more deeply on rival accounts of parental authority, human nature, moral formation, or the reach of government. A dispute about religious liberty may appear to concern one statute while resting on different accounts of conscience, equality, and institutional authority.

Naming those assumptions does not make every position equally plausible. Quite the opposite. Once assumptions become visible, they can themselves face examination. Do they cohere? Do they explain the evidence? Do they contradict other commitments? Can their consequences survive scrutiny?

Facts still matter

Recognizing background assumptions should never become an excuse for relativism. Some claims remain false. Some evidence remains stronger than other evidence. Some arguments contain contradictions, unsupported causal claims, or invalid inferences. Serious inquiry therefore has to avoid two opposite mistakes: pretending facts interpret themselves, and pretending interpretation makes facts irrelevant.

A stronger question than “What evidence supports this conclusion?” asks, “What premises connect the evidence to the conclusion?” That second question often reveals why intelligent people can examine the same record and see very different things.
