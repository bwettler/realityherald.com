Reality Check: What Does “Studies Show” Actually Tell You?

A five-question test for research claims that arrive prepackaged as certainty.

RH
Reality Herald Editorial Desk September 2026 · 5 min read
Filed under Reality CheckScience & Technology
Article type Reality Check

“Studies show” sounds impressive because the phrase borrows the authority of research without telling the reader what research actually found.

The phrase may introduce excellent evidence. It may also conceal a weak study, an irrelevant finding, an observational correlation presented as causation, or a result so qualified that the headline no longer resembles the paper.

Ask: which study?

A serious claim should allow the reader to locate the evidence. Who conducted the research? Where was it published? What population was studied? How large was the sample? What outcome did the researchers actually measure?

Those questions often reveal that a sweeping shared claim rests on a much narrower finding.

Ask: what did the study compare?

A study may find that two variables move together without establishing that one caused the other. People who exercise more may differ from people who exercise less in many ways. Schools using one curriculum may serve populations different from schools using another. Social-science research often tries to control for such differences, but controls do not automatically eliminate every alternative explanation.

Ask: how large was the effect?

Statistical significance does not necessarily mean practical importance. A very large sample can make a tiny difference statistically detectable. Conversely, an important effect may fail to reach conventional significance in a small study.

Readers should therefore look for effect size, uncertainty, replication, and whether the outcome matters outside the statistical model.

Ask: does the headline match the paper?

Researchers often write cautiously. Headlines often do not. Words such as “associated with,” “may,” “suggests,” and “in this sample” can disappear as research moves through press releases and social media.

Reality checking begins by walking the claim backward: headline, article, press release, abstract, paper, data if available.

Good skepticism is specific

Dismissing all research as biased is not serious skepticism. Neither is accepting a claim because the word “study” appears nearby. Better skepticism asks exactly what the evidence establishes and exactly where inference begins.

Reality Check: When someone says “studies show,” ask for the strongest specific study and read the conclusion in the authors' own words.
