TEACHER RESOURCES · WEBQUESTS

Build a WebQuest around a question worth answering.

A useful WebQuest does more than send students searching for facts. The assignment gives students a problem, curates credible starting sources, requires them to compare claims, and asks them to produce a reasoned conclusion.

01

Introduction

Frame a genuine problem or controversy. Give students enough context to understand why the question matters without deciding the answer for them.

02

Task

Define the final product: argument essay, debate brief, committee resolution, mock-trial memorandum, presentation, source analysis, or another concrete outcome.

03

Process

Specify the research stages. Require students to identify competing positions, evaluate sources, distinguish evidence from interpretation, and answer objections.

04

Sources

Provide a curated starting set while allowing students to expand beyond it. Mix primary sources, serious journalism, scholarly work, and clearly identified advocacy where useful.

05

Evaluation

Grade the quality of reasoning as well as factual accuracy. A rubric can assess thesis, evidence, source quality, counterargument, organization, and intellectual fairness.

06

Conclusion

Ask students what changed in their thinking, which evidence mattered most, and which questions remain unresolved.

SAMPLE REALITY HERALD WEBQUEST

Why can people agree on evidence and still disagree about conclusions?

Task: Read a Reality Herald argument article, identify the author's thesis and unstated premises, locate two serious opposing sources, then write a response explaining which disagreement concerns evidence and which concerns underlying assumptions.

Extension: Convert the research into a five-minute debate speech or a one-page position brief.
